Vision

Understanding intelligence beyond familiar boundaries.

Intelligence usually means brains. Yet a root or a fungal network responds to its surroundings with no nervous system at all, and an ant colony coordinates thousands of individuals with no central control. These are different kinds of organization, and sensing and responding are not the same as intelligence. But they can be measured, and that makes them a place to start.

Auriora is an open research initiative that takes this seriously. We are building open instruments to record how plants signal and respond, and we develop them in public. Whether the word intelligence reaches that far is a result we are after, not an assumption we start from.

The question

What is intelligence, and how does it emerge in different systems?

Most of us think we know intelligence when we see it. Try to say what actually makes a system intelligent, though, and the answer gets slippery fast.

A dog that has been to the vet once will refuse the car the next time. A bean seedling turns toward the window within a day, and its root changes course when it meets a stone. An ant colony has no one in charge, yet it reroutes when the path to food is blocked. Nothing about these cases looks alike.

What they share is harder to name. Each takes in something from its surroundings and changes what it does because of it. Whether that deserves the same word in every case is exactly what we do not know.

What, if anything, do these systems have in common?
And is it the same thing we mean when we say intelligence?

An open question

Why do we still struggle to explain intelligence?

Partly because most of what we know comes from humans and other animals, and concepts built around nervous systems travel badly to a root tip or a mycelium.

Partly because the measurements are hard to compare. Plant signaling has been studied experimentally for more than a century, but a recording depends on the electrode, the plant, the room and the stimulus, and unless all of that is documented, one study cannot easily be checked against the next.

So there is still no agreed answer to what intelligence is, whether it comes down to one mechanism, or why it shows up where it does. We do not expect that answer to come from arguing about definitions. It will come from observations that can be compared and repeated, made in the open.

Principles

How do we explore the unknown?

We measure before we interpret. The recording comes first, and the explanation is allowed to change when the next recording disagrees with it.

Everything we make is meant to stay open: the instrument designs, the methods, the raw data and the reasoning that connects them. If a result cannot be reproduced from what we publish, we treat that as a bug.

Careful engineering is not a separate concern from careful science. A signal is only as trustworthy as the tool that recorded it.

Where we focus

Starting where the signals are measurable.

If intelligence is more widespread than our categories assume, the place to find out is where the signals can actually be recorded.

Auriora begins with plants, whose signals can be recorded with instruments we can build and share. The fungi and networks around them come later. We do not begin by assuming that plants are intelligent and then set out to prove it. We begin with what these systems measurably do, and let the evidence decide what the word means here.

Electrical signaling

Action, variation and systemic potentials: the fast, long-distance signals that travel through living tissue.

Chemical signaling

Volatile and chemical cues between plants and their surroundings. A later direction, not one we measure yet.

Light and photobiology

How living systems read the spectrum, direction and timing of light, and reshape themselves in response.

Sound and vibration

Mechanical and acoustic signals as stimuli, and potentially as information the organism acts on.

Direction

Beyond familiar definitions.

Plants are the starting point, not the boundary.

Once the methods are reliable in plants, the interesting question is how other living systems sense, respond and adapt, from fungal networks and colonies to lineages that parted ways with plants more than a billion years ago. A familiar-looking signal is only a clue, and what it does for the organism matters more. The similarities will be telling. So will the differences.

Auriora is not a theory of intelligence. It is a place to work on the question in public, with instruments and data anyone can check.

What we are building

Instruments for open exploration.

A question this open needs instruments and repeatable results. Auriora is developing an open, modular platform for measuring and stimulating living systems in step, so that measurement, stimulation and recording form one documented experiment.

The first modules in development record plant electrical signals and stimulate with light and sound, built to run together. They are a starting point, not the scope. The platform is meant to grow as new research questions call for new instruments.

Everything is built in the open under one engineering standard for hardware, firmware, software and documentation. Reproducibility is concrete: open designs, versioned interfaces, documented calibration, and timing recorded with the data, so an experiment can be repeated, not merely described. The standards are already public, and the instrument repositories follow as each module is ready for release.

If you study plant or network behavior, these instruments are being built for you to use, question and improve. To take part, write to contact@auriora.org.
